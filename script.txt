You are nineteen years old. The letter arrived three weeks ago. Your mother presses a small tin cross into your palm at the train station — the same one your father carried in the last war, and never used to save himself. "Bring it back," she says. Not "come back." Bring it back. In six hours, you'll be on the Eastern Front.

As a Schütze — a private — your world shrinks to about ten meters in every direction. You carry sixty pounds of gear: rifle, ammunition, entrenching tool, rations that never last long enough. The tin cross stays in your breast pocket, over your heart, because it's the only thing in your kit that isn't army-issued. You dig. You march. You wait. The average private on the Eastern Front has a life expectancy measured in weeks, not months. You watch men beside you disappear in an afternoon. You don't ask their names anymore. It's easier that way. You touch the cross before every advance. You don't tell anyone why.

You survive the winter. Somehow, you're still standing when the spring offensive begins. Command notices. You're promoted.

As an Unteroffizier, you now lead a squad of eight men — most of them younger than you were six months ago. The weight on your shoulders changes. It's no longer just your own life in the balance. You give the order to advance. You give the order to hold. Sometimes both orders get men killed, and there's no way to know beforehand which one it will be. One night, a boy named Brandt asks what the cross around your neck is. You tell him it's nothing. He doesn't believe you. Neither do you.

The counterattack comes at dawn. You hold the line when others don't. Command notices again.

As a Leutnant, you command a platoon — thirty, sometimes forty men. You're an officer now, which means you eat separately, sleep separately, and carry a pistol instead of a rifle. The isolation sets in. Your soldiers don't tell you what they're really afraid of anymore. You plan attacks on maps that don't show the mud, the cold, or the sound a man makes when he doesn't survive a crossing. The cross is scratched now, the paint worn from your thumb running over it the same way, every single night.

You take the position. Casualties are "acceptable," they tell you. You don't feel acceptable about any of it. But you've proven something. Command notices, one more time.

As a Hauptmann, you command a company — over a hundred men, spread across a front line you can no longer see all at once. You've stopped fighting with your hands. Now you fight with information — troop movements, supply lines, terrain reports that arrive too late to matter. During a retreat under artillery fire, you feel for the cross and your pocket is empty. You go back for it. Your adjutant screams at you to leave it. You don't. You find it half-buried in the mud where you fell three days earlier, and you put it back over your heart before you even check if you're bleeding.

Of the men who served on the Eastern Front, historians estimate that roughly a third never made it home. Rank didn't guarantee survival — it just changed what you were responsible for while the war decided your fate. You make it home in the spring of 1945, thinner, older than your years, the tin cross still in your pocket, its edges worn smooth. Your mother doesn't ask if you kept your promise. She just takes your hand and feels it there, still warm from your chest.

Every rank carried its own war within the war. Some men carried something smaller than that, all the way through it.
